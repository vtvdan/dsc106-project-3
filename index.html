Polar Amplification: Latitudinal Asymmetry in Projected Surface Warming

The Arctic is warming much faster than the rest of the planet, a pattern called polar amplification. Pick an emissions scenario and move the year to see how much faster. Hover the chart to read any latitude band.

Each point on the line is a band of latitude (north is up). The farther right it sits, the more that band has warmed since 1995-2014. The straight dashed vertical line is the global average, and the shaded area is the gap from it.

Write-up

Motivation

We chose this topic because of the stark difference in warming in the arctic compared to the rest of the world we found in our exploratory visualizations. We also recognized the significant impact arctic warming will have, leading to a rising sea level and other environmental effects. A viewer of this visualization should see how warming plays out over time and in different scenarios and see the particularly large impact on the arctic.

Design Decisions

We first transformed the raw monthly data by averaging it into annual values so we can compare it across different years. We then chose to use the period from 1995-2014 as our historical baseline because those years are the last 20 years of the models' historical runs, preceding the future projections. By using each model's own historical baseline, we can compare changes in warming. We also chose to average across longitude because it removes any unnecessary geographic detail, especially considering how our research question focuses specifically on latitude. The 5° latitude bands help us reduce visual clutter and compact a large dataset to fit within a webpage. Averaging across the 3 CMIP6 models helps reduce any influence from the predictions of each model. While this makes the warming pattern more consistent overall, we do recognize that having only 3 models isn't a big enough sample size to fully capture the climate model uncertainty.

Averaging over an 11-year window helps us to not only make long-term warming trends more visible by reducing short-term temperature fluctuations, but also prevent the time slider from jittering while being dragged. Doing this, however, comes at the expense of obscuring those short-term weather variations. Finally, we chose to have a cos(latitude) weighting so that latitude bands near the poles would not disproportionately influence the global average, given how much less surface area is covered by those bands compared to ones near the equator. This helps make the Arctic-to-global warming ratio more balanced and representative.

We wanted our visualization to convey how warming would change over time, but we also wanted to show that latitude influenced the degree of warming. Since our data gives us warming projections in each year at many latitudes, we decided to use a curved line plot displaying the projected warming at each latitude. We decided to have latitude on the vertical axis rather than the horizontal axis because, on a 2D map, latitude is on the vertical axis. We decided to use different colors to represent the different emissions scenarios, escalating from blue to orange to red to represent the change to a scenario with more warming. We also have those scenarios as interactive buttons to allow users to switch between different emission pathways, keeping the other two visible as dashed lines for easy comparison. The solid and dashed lines inside each button correspond to the scenario being shown, conveniently serving as a legend.

We also shaded the area between each scenario-specific line and the dashed global-average line to show how far each latitude is from the global average. In addition, we wanted regions like the Arctic and tropics to stand out on the graph, so those latitude ranges are slightly highlighted. We included useful stats like the Arctic's warming, the global warming, and their ratio on the right side for the viewer to easily reference.

To convey change over time, we use an interactive slider which changes the year from 2015 to 2100, and dragging it changes the graph, the description, and the statistics of the selected scenario. The Play button moves the slider on its own, playing an animation of the warming projections throughout that time frame (2015-2100). This helps users visualize how those patterns develop over time, especially focusing on how the Arctic is pulling away from other regions. Having this along with the slider allows users to control what they see and digest information at their own pace, something that a static chart can't do. We also allow the viewer to hover over specific points to get the data at that time and latitude, specifically the overall warming and how many times greater it is than the global average.

Alternatives Considered

We considered other visualizations such as using a heat map as our first static visualization, but adding an interactive element to see how heat evolved over time. We chose not to go forward with this because it wouldn't really effectively display the magnitude of difference between the poles and other locations. We also considered using interactions over latitude, however we believed that conveying change over time using interaction was more intuitive and better from a viewer's perspective. We also thought about potentially using the ratio between Arctic temperature and a latitude's temperature as the main variable, like in our sixth static visualization. However, we thought it wasn't the most intuitive as a basic unit so we only included it as a summary stat and have a comparison to the global average when you hover over the point.

Development Process

The strategy to attack this project was to divide up the work evenly. Daniel set up the repository to get started and did the data processing step. This included loading in the CMIP6 data and averaging it into zonal warming for each emission scenario. He then had to export the cleaned CSVs and make the first two exploratory graphs. Karl made graph 3 while also recording our checkpoint video and contributing to the write up by addressing our motivations, design decisions and alternatives. Ananya made graphs four to six which helped explore more parts of the data, allowing us to come to a conclusion on what our final visualization would look like and describe. The graphs pushed us to change our research question. It first questioned latitudinal asymmetry then we were able to find a nicher approach and alter it to directly compare the Arctic with the global average temperatures. Daniel built the interactive visualization in D3 and did the later rounds of styling. All three of us discussed which design to go with based on the exploratory graphs and what would make a strong visualization from everything we have learned so far in class.

The most time consuming part was the data processing at the start, since the raw climate model files are large and needed to be combined across models. They also needed to be averaged by latitude before we could plot anything. The second part was the interactive visualization itself which was the biggest hurdle of this project. Getting the slider, the scenario buttons, the hover tooltip, and the summary statistics to all work together took a lot of tries and rounds of revision. The part in the code that we most struggled with was making sure the graph, statistics, and captions all updated correctly when the year or the emission scenario changed. The exploratory static graphs were easier and moved quicker which was the same situation with the write-up.
